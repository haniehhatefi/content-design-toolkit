## 1. Research question

How does voice consistency break down across a content set, and what changes in alignment are observable following human-guided revision?

---

## 2. Hypothesis

AI-generated content, when produced piece by piece without explicit cross-piece consistency requirements, is likely to show register and structural inconsistencies across a content set — particularly between content types that perform different communicative functions (instructional, confirmational, empty state). These inconsistencies are likely to be more visible at the register and structural level than at the lexical level.

Human-guided revision will produce observable changes in alignment but will also reveal cases where the voice framework does not resolve the choice between two acceptable approaches — where practitioner judgment informed by product context, rather than the framework alone, determines the final register.

---

## 3. Test scenario

**Screen:** A notification preferences screen for a generic web application.

**Content task:** A practitioner has been asked to produce UX copy for a notification preferences screen. The content set covers five distinct content types that appear on the same screen:

| Piece | Content type | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| P1 | Page heading | Identifies the screen |
| P2 | Helper text | Provides contextual instruction below the heading |
| P3 | Toggle sub-label | Describes the effect of an individual notification toggle |
| P4 | Confirmation toast | Confirms that settings have been saved |
| P5 | Empty state message | Appears when no notifications are enabled |

The initial content set is a constructed illustrative example representing content that might result from AI-assisted drafting without explicit cross-piece consistency requirements.

This scenario is distinct from Phase 4's worked examples, which demonstrate voice alignment review on a single component (five button labels for one function). This experiment tests consistency across a full screen covering different content types that require different communicative registers — a more demanding and realistic condition.

---

## 4. Materials

| Document | Role |
|---|---|
| `01-foundations/voice-principles.md` | Establishes the three voice attributes and two cross-cutting writing standards (Clear, Human, Helpful, Direct, Consistent) used to evaluate each piece |
| `02-ux-writing-standards/` | Provides UX writing standards for specific content types: confirmations, empty states, helper text |
| `03-ai-content-creation/worked-examples.md` | Provides the output evaluation rubric and establishes the Phase 4 convention for voice alignment review — the precedent this experiment builds on |

**Evaluation dimensions:** This experiment applies the five evaluation dimensions from Phase 2 — three voice attributes (Clear, Human, Helpful) and two cross-cutting writing standards (Direct, Consistent) — as the primary evaluation framework. The evaluation does not use a scoring system. It identifies specific instances where a piece does or does not meet a dimension, and notes where inconsistency exists across the set as a whole.

---

## 5. Procedure

How the illustrative case is constructed, examined, and evaluated, step by step:

1. **Establish evaluation criteria** — confirm the five evaluation dimensions (three voice attributes: Clear, Human, Helpful; and two cross-cutting writing standards: Direct, Consistent) and any content-type-specific standards from Phase 3 that apply to this content set.
2. **Present the initial content set** — the five illustrative pieces as generated by AI assistance, without consistency review.
3. **Evaluate each piece independently** — apply the five evaluation dimensions to each piece in isolation.
4. **Evaluate the set as a whole** — identify inconsistencies across pieces, particularly register, structural, and framing inconsistencies that only become visible when pieces are read together.
5. **Conduct a human-guided revision pass** — revise each piece that did not meet one or more dimensions, documenting the decisions made at each step.
6. **Re-evaluate the revised set** — apply the same criteria to the revised pieces.
7. **Document remaining issues** — identify cases where revision resolved the finding, cases where revision produced an improvement but left an open question, and cases where the finding required ongoing editorial judgment rather than a single revision.

## 6. Observations

What the application of the method identifies, including deliberate weaknesses, evaluation findings, and human intervention points.

### 6.1 Initial content set

*Illustrative output — constructed to demonstrate register and structural inconsistency across a multi-piece content set. Deliberate weaknesses have been introduced as noted.*

| Piece | Content type | Initial output |
|---|---|---|
| P1 | Page heading | Notification Preferences |
| P2 | Helper text | Here you can manage your notification settings and configure how and when you would like to receive updates from the platform. |
| P3 | Toggle sub-label | Enable email notifications to be informed about important account activity, security alerts, and product updates on a timely basis. |
| P4 | Confirmation toast | Your notification preferences have been successfully updated. |
| P5 | Empty state | Looks like notifications are off. Turn them on to stay up to date with what's happening on your account. |

**Deliberate weaknesses introduced:** Each piece reflects a plausible output from AI-assisted generation in the absence of explicit consistency instructions. The register across the set is not uniform: Pieces 2 and 4 use formal constructions; Piece 5 uses a casual conversational opener. Piece 3 applies an instructional framing pattern that is structurally inappropriate for its content type. These are realistic failure modes when content is generated piece by piece without a register model or cross-piece consistency requirement.

---

### 6.2 Evaluation against voice principles

Each piece is evaluated against the five evaluation dimensions. A finding of **does not meet** at a given dimension identifies a specific issue. **Meets** does not mean the piece is final — it means no issue was identified at this dimension.

#### P1 — Page heading: "Notification Preferences"

| Dimension | Assessment | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Clear | Meets | Unambiguous category label |
| Human | Meets | Neutral; appropriate register for a heading |
| Helpful | — | Not applicable: headings identify rather than guide |
| Direct | Meets | No filler language |
| Consistent | — | Establishes the visual and categorical register of the screen; cross-piece consistency cannot be assessed from a heading alone |

P1 not revised.

---

#### P2 — Helper text: "Here you can manage your notification settings and configure how and when you would like to receive updates from the platform."

| Dimension | Assessment | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Clear | Meets | The purpose of the screen is communicated |
| Human | Does not meet | "you would like to receive" is subjunctive and formal; "the platform" is impersonal corporate language |
| Helpful | Meets | Contextualises what the screen does |
| Direct | Does not meet | "Here you can" is an unnecessary preamble; the sentence largely restates the heading rather than adding new information |
| Consistent | Does not meet | Formal register ("you would like to receive", "the platform") is inconsistent with Piece 5's conversational register |

**Key finding:** Two directness issues and a formality issue. "The platform" creates distance between the product and the user — it refers to the product in the third person rather than addressing the relationship directly.

---

#### P3 — Toggle sub-label: "Enable email notifications to be informed about important account activity, security alerts, and product updates on a timely basis."

| Dimension | Assessment | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Clear | Does not meet | The instructional framing ("Enable...to be informed") reads as instruction rather than description; a toggle sub-label describes what the toggle does, not what the user must do |
| Human | Does not meet | "to be informed" is passive and formal; "on a timely basis" is filler |
| Helpful | Meets | The scope of notifications (account activity, security alerts, product updates) is relevant and informative |
| Direct | Does not meet | "on a timely basis" adds nothing; the construction is longer than its content requires |
| Consistent | Does not meet | The instructional pattern ("Enable...to [outcome]") differs structurally from the descriptive pattern that would be consistent with other pieces on the screen |

**Key finding:** Structural mismatch with content type. A toggle sub-label describes the effect of enabling the toggle — what will happen — not the action the user must take. The instructional framing misapplies a CTA pattern to a descriptive content type.

---

#### P4 — Confirmation toast: "Your notification preferences have been successfully updated."

| Dimension | Assessment | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Clear | Meets | Communicates that the action was completed |
| Human | Does not meet | Passive construction ("have been...updated") distances the product from the action; "successfully" is redundant corporate language |
| Helpful | Meets | Confirms the outcome the user expected |
| Direct | Does not meet | Passive voice and "successfully" make the message longer and more formal than a toast requires |
| Consistent | Does not meet | Formal register is inconsistent with Piece 5 |

**Key finding:** "Successfully" and passive voice are recurring AI-output tendencies in confirmation copy. Neither adds information — the confirmation only appears if the action succeeded, and passive voice attributes the update to an unnamed agent. This piece requires the most direct revision of the set.

---

#### P5 — Empty state: "Looks like notifications are off. Turn them on to stay up to date with what's happening on your account."

| Dimension | Assessment | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Clear | Meets | Communicates the current state and the available action |
| Human | Meets | Conversational; uses active voice throughout |
| Helpful | Meets | Provides a reason to act ("stay up to date") and a clear call to action |
| Direct | Meets | No filler language; second sentence is actionable |
| Consistent | Does not meet | The casual opener ("Looks like") introduces a register notably more informal than Pieces 2 and 4 |

**Key finding:** Piece 5 is the strongest piece by most dimensions but the most inconsistent with the rest of the set. The register gap is in the opposite direction from the other inconsistencies — the problem is not that Piece 5 is too formal, but that it is more conversational than the set as a whole.

---

### 6.3 Consistency findings across the set

| Inconsistency type | Pieces affected | Description |
|---|---|---|
| Register: formal vs. conversational | P2, P4 (formal) vs. P5 (conversational) | No consistent register established across the set |
| Active vs. passive voice | P4 (passive) vs. P2, P3, P5 (active or mixed) | P4 uses passive construction; other pieces do not |
| Structural/framing pattern | P3 (instructional) vs. P2, P5 (descriptive/informational) | P3 applies a CTA framing pattern inappropriate for its content type |
| Verbosity relative to content type | P2, P3 (verbose) vs. P4, P5 (proportionate) | Helper text and toggle sub-label are longer than their content types warrant |

**Summary:** Four inconsistency types across five pieces. The most significant issues are (1) the register gap between Pieces 2 and 4 and Piece 5, and (2) the structural mismatch in Piece 3.

---

### 6.4 Human-guided revision pass

The practitioner reviewed the evaluation findings and made revisions. The register decision — whether to bring the formal pieces toward Piece 5's register, or Piece 5 toward the formal pieces, or to establish a new midpoint — required explicit practitioner judgment. The voice framework's "Human" principle favours a warmer, more conversational register. This supported revising toward Piece 5's register rather than away from it.

**Practitioner judgment call — register target:** The choice of register target is not resolvable by the framework alone. It depends on the product's established voice: if an existing voice and tone guide is in place, the register decision is anchored there. For this experiment, the practitioner applied the "Human" principle to favour the conversational end of the spectrum. This decision should be documented in the content brief so future contributors understand what register was chosen and why.

---

#### Revisions

**P2 — Helper text**

*Initial:* "Here you can manage your notification settings and configure how and when you would like to receive updates from the platform."

*Revised:* "Manage when and how you hear from us."

Decision notes:
- "Here you can" preamble removed
- "hear from us" replaces "receive updates from the platform" — first person ("us") is more human than "the platform"; "hear from" is more natural than "receive updates from"
- Condensed substantially: the initial sentence restated the heading; the revised sentence adds the concept of control ("Manage when and how") without repeating the screen's subject
- **Judgment call:** "us" vs. the product name vs. "the app" — the practitioner chose "us" as the most human option, but this choice depends on whether the product uses first-person plural voice consistently elsewhere. If it does not, "the app" or the product name would be more consistent with the broader voice.

**P3 — Toggle sub-label**

*Initial:* "Enable email notifications to be informed about important account activity, security alerts, and product updates on a timely basis."

*Revised:* "Get updates about your account, security alerts, and new features by email."

Decision notes:
- Reframed from instructional ("Enable...to be informed") to descriptive ("Get updates...by email") — appropriate for the content type
- "product updates" revised to "new features" — more concrete and human
- "on a timely basis" removed entirely — filler with no content value
- **Judgment call:** Whether to use "Get updates" (action-framed description) or "Sends you updates" (product as subject) — both are descriptive rather than instructional. The practitioner chose "Get" to maintain the user-centric framing consistent with the rest of the set. "Sends you updates" would also be acceptable and might be preferred in a product with a more product-centric voice.
- **Scope note:** The revised copy intends that all three categories — account updates, security alerts, and new features — are delivered by email. This is the correct reading of the construction. If any category were delivered by a different channel, the copy would need to be revised to reflect that.

**P4 — Confirmation toast**

*Initial:* "Your notification preferences have been successfully updated."

*Revised:* "Preferences saved."

Decision notes:
- Passive voice converted to implied active construction — the subject is omitted, which is standard toast convention
- "successfully" removed — redundant in a confirmation context; a toast only appears if the action succeeded
- "notification preferences" shortened to "Preferences" — contextually clear on the notification preferences screen; abbreviated forms are appropriate for toasts
- **Judgment call:** "Preferences saved." vs. "Notification preferences saved." — the practitioner chose the shorter form on the basis that the screen context makes the reference clear. In a product that surfaces toasts across many different screens, the more specific form might be safer.

**P5 — Empty state**

*Initial:* "Looks like notifications are off. Turn them on to stay up to date with what's happening on your account."

*Revised:* "No notifications enabled yet. Update your preferences to stay up to date."

Decision notes:
- Casual opener "Looks like" removed — the revised first sentence states the state directly
- "yet" retained — implies the state is temporary and that enabling notifications is expected; appropriate for an empty state
- "what's happening on your account" shortened to "stay up to date" — the longer phrase was vague; the shorter form retains the motivation to act without introducing a loose construction
- **Judgment call:** Whether to retain "yet" — it adds a slight tone of expectation that may not fit all product voices. The practitioner retained it on the grounds that it is a light signal appropriate for an empty state. If the product uses a neutral or more factual voice, "yet" should be removed.

### 6.6 Re-evaluation: consistency across the revised set

| Inconsistency type | Status after revision | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Register: formal vs. conversational | Substantially resolved | All pieces now use a conversational register. P4 ("Preferences saved.") is terse, but this reflects the toast content type — contextual variation rather than inconsistency |
| Active vs. passive voice | Resolved | All pieces use active constructions or convention-appropriate abbreviated forms (P4) |
| Structural/framing pattern | Resolved | P3 revised from instructional to descriptive |
| Product reference | Impersonal reference removed; product-level voice consistency unverified | "The platform" no longer appears in the revised set. Whether "us" in P2 is consistent with the product's broader voice cannot be confirmed from this content set alone — see open question below |
| Verbosity relative to content type | Resolved | Helper text and toggle sub-label are now proportionate to their content types |

**Remaining open questions after revision:**

1. **First-person plural voice:** "us" in P2 reflects a first-person plural voice choice. If the product does not use first-person plural elsewhere, this introduces a new inconsistency at the product level rather than the screen level. This cannot be resolved from the content set alone — it requires either an existing voice guide or a brief decision recorded before content creation begins.

2. **Empty state framing:** "No notifications enabled yet" states the negative current state. Some practitioners would prefer a positive-action framing ("Turn on notifications to stay up to date"). Both are consistent with the revised register; they reflect different conventions for how empty states acknowledge current state vs. invite action. This is an open editorial question that the voice framework does not resolve.

3. **Toggle sub-label scope:** "Get updates about your account, security alerts, and new features by email" lists three categories. If the product's notification types change, this copy needs updating. Whether to describe scope specifically (risking staleness) or generically ("Get email updates") is a maintenance tradeoff not resolvable from the voice framework alone.

## 7. Evaluation

### What the method identified

The five-principle evaluation framework identified the most significant issues in the initial set. The dimensions most useful for identifying cross-piece inconsistency were **Human** and **Consistent** — they surfaced the register gap between Pieces 2 and 4 and Piece 5, and the passive construction in Piece 4. The **Direct** dimension was most useful for identifying piece-level issues (verbosity, filler language) that would not have surfaced from a cross-piece consistency check alone. The **Clear** dimension identified the structural mismatch in Piece 3 — specifically, that the instructional framing was inappropriate for a toggle sub-label's communicative function.

### What the method did not resolve

The framework establishes a direction (more human, more conversational, more direct) but does not specify a register target. When pieces in the same set diverge in register, the practitioner must decide which register to standardise toward. This choice depends on product context that the framework cannot supply.

For this content set, the practitioner had three defensible options:
- Revise toward Piece 5's conversational register *(chosen)*
- Revise toward a midpoint between Pieces 2 and 4 and Piece 5
- Establish a new register position not represented in the initial set

Each option produces a consistently voiced result. The evaluation method identifies the inconsistency and the direction of improvement; it does not eliminate the practitioner's decision about where to land.

### Contextual variation vs. systemic inconsistency

One finding illustrates a distinction the evaluation must make. The revised confirmation toast ("Preferences saved.") is terse and structurally abbreviated compared to the helper text and empty state. A toast conventionally omits the subject and uses a past-tense completion form. This is structurally different from the other pieces, but the difference is contextually appropriate rather than inconsistent. The evaluation method must distinguish between variation that serves the content type and variation that reflects systemic inconsistency across equivalent content types.

### Human intervention points

Three categories of human intervention occurred during this experiment. These are distinct from the four specific judgment calls documented in Section 6.4, which sit within the third category below.

1. **Framework application** — identifying which pieces met or did not meet which dimensions. This can be supported by AI but requires practitioner knowledge of content-type conventions (for example, recognising that P3's instructional framing was inappropriate for a toggle sub-label is a content design judgment, not a checklist item).

2. **Register target decision** — choosing which register to standardise toward. This requires product context and practitioner judgment. The framework supports but does not make this decision.

3. **Individual judgment calls during revision** — word-level decisions that the framework does not resolve. The four specific calls are documented in Section 6.4; each involved a choice between two or more defensible options, none of which the framework ranked.

## 8. Limitations

**Illustrative output**

The initial content set was constructed to demonstrate specific inconsistency patterns. This experiment cannot establish that an AI system would produce these specific inconsistencies under equivalent conditions, or that providing explicit consistency instructions would have prevented them. The comparison between the initial set and the revised set documents the method's application to two illustrative cases. It does not constitute evidence of the method's effectiveness under real generation conditions.

**Small content set**

Five pieces covering one screen is a small test case. A full product content set may have dozens of pieces across many screens, produced over time by different practitioners. The method's ability to identify consistency issues across a larger set, and to maintain consistency over time as content is added or revised, is not tested here.

**Register target as external knowledge**

The register decision relied on the practitioner's judgment to favour the "Human" principle and the more conversational register. In practice, this decision is anchored by an existing voice guide — a document the content brief should reference. This experiment assumes a practitioner familiar enough with the product's voice to make this call. Where no such context exists, the method identifies the inconsistency but requires the register decision to be established as a brief-level requirement before the method can resolve it.

**Single content type combination**

This experiment uses one specific combination of content types (heading, helper text, toggle sub-label, confirmation toast, empty state) for one screen type (settings/preferences). The specific inconsistency patterns that emerged reflect these content types. Other combinations — a marketing landing page, a checkout flow, an error screen — would produce different patterns. The method generalises; the specific findings from this combination do not.

## 9. Recommendations

### 1. Include register guidance in the content brief

The most significant finding from this experiment is that the voice framework identifies register inconsistency but does not resolve the register target decision. The content brief's requirements section should specify the target register, with a reference example from approved content. This prevents the register decision from being made implicitly during generation or review.

*Toolkit implication:* The content brief template could include a "Register model" field under Requirements — a brief description of the target register and an optional reference example. This is a low-cost addition that would have resolved the main ambiguity in this experiment before content creation began.

### 2. Use an anchor piece when generating multi-piece content sets

When using AI assistance to produce a content set covering multiple types, provide an anchor piece as part of the generation input — an example of the approved register — and include an explicit instruction to maintain register consistency across the set. The initial set was constructed without an explicit cross-piece consistency requirement, illustrating why register guidance and an anchor example should be included when preparing multi-piece content tasks.

*Toolkit implication:* Phase 4 prompting guidance could include a note on cross-piece consistency for multi-piece content tasks: specifically, that the prompt should include a register model and an instruction to match it across all pieces.

### 3. Distinguish contextual variation from systemic inconsistency in evaluation

The evaluation framework should be applied with awareness of content-type conventions. A confirmation toast being terser than helper text is contextually appropriate — that is how toasts behave. The evaluation is looking for cases where the same register is used inconsistently across equivalent content types, or where the voice shifts without a clear content-type reason.

*Toolkit implication:* Phase 2 voice framework documentation could include a brief note on how the Consistent dimension applies across different content types — specifically, that parallel content types (multiple toasts, multiple empty states) should share a register, while structurally different types may appropriately differ.

### 4. Document judgment calls in the decision log

The revision pass produced four explicit judgment calls that the voice framework did not resolve:

- **P2:** "us" vs. the product name or "the app" — first-person plural voice decision with implications across the product
- **P3:** "Get updates" (user-centric framing) vs. "Sends you updates" (product-centric framing)
- **P4:** "Preferences saved." vs. "Notification preferences saved." — abbreviation vs. specificity in a toast
- **P5:** retaining or removing "yet" depending on the product's register and tone

These should be recorded in the decision and iteration log as practitioner decisions — with the rationale — so that future contributors to the same content set understand what choices were made and why. All four have implications beyond a single piece: first-person voice, framing convention, content-type shorthand, and register markers each recur across a content set.

*Toolkit implication:* This is consistent with existing Phase 5 documentation practice. The finding reinforces the importance of maintaining the decision log for voice decisions, not only structural or compliance decisions.
