# Tone spectrum

Voice is consistent. Tone adapts.

Your product's voice — the five attributes described in [voice principles](voice-principles.md) — should be recognisable across all content. Tone is how those attributes express themselves given a specific context: who the reader is, what they are trying to do, and what emotional state they are likely in.

---

## Voice vs. tone: the distinction

| | Voice | Tone |
|---|---|---|
| **What it is** | The stable personality of the product | How that personality adapts to context |
| **Changes with context?** | No | Yes |
| **Decided by** | Brand, product, and content standards | The specific situation and user state |
| **Example** | The product is always clear and human | A confirmation message is warm and brief; an error message is calm and practical |

A useful analogy: a person has a consistent character — the way they think and what they value — but they don't speak the same way in a job interview as they do with a close friend. The character is stable; the expression adapts.

---

## Two axes of tone

Tone can be mapped against two axes. These are not scales to maximise or minimise — they are dials to adjust based on context.

### Axis 1: Formal ↔ Warm

| Formal | Warm |
|---|---|
| Precise, structured, professional | Conversational, personal, approachable |
| Uses complete sentences and standard grammar | May use fragments or contractions |
| Appropriate for: legal notices, security alerts, regulated processes | Appropriate for: onboarding, celebrations, empty states |

### Axis 2: Calm ↔ Encouraging

| Calm | Encouraging |
|---|---|
| Neutral, factual, low affect | Energising, affirming, forward-looking |
| Appropriate for: errors, warnings, waiting states | Appropriate for: task completion, first-use moments, progress milestones |

## Tone by context

The table below maps common interface contexts to tone position and illustrates the difference in practice.

| Context | Tone | Why | Illustrative example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **First use / onboarding** | Warm, encouraging | The reader is new and forming their first impression | *"Let's get you set up. This takes about 2 minutes."* |
| **Task completion (low stakes)** | Warm, calm | Acknowledge without overdoing it | *"Done. Your [item] has been saved."* |
| **Task completion (significant milestone)** | Warm, encouraging | The reader has done something that took effort | *"You've completed [milestone]. Here's what's next."* |
| **Error (recoverable)** | Calm, helpful | The reader is frustrated; don't add to it | *"Something went wrong. Check your connection and try again."* |
| **Error (data loss or unrecoverable)** | Calm, honest | Urgency without alarm; honesty without blame | *"We couldn't save your changes. The last saved version is from [time]."* |
| **Destructive action warning** | Calm, direct | The reader needs to make an informed decision | *"This will permanently delete [item]. You can't undo this."* |
| **Empty state (first use)** | Warm, inviting | Invite action rather than describe absence | *"Nothing here yet. [CTA to add first item]."* |
| **Empty state (no results)** | Calm, helpful | Practical rather than apologetic | *"No results for '[term]'. Try a different word or phrase."* |
| **Waiting / loading** | Calm, honest | Manage expectations; avoid false cheerfulness | *"This usually takes about 30 seconds."* |
| **Security / compliance notice** | Formal, calm | Precision matters; warmth is secondary here | *"Your session has expired. Sign in again to continue."* |
| **Maintenance / downtime** | Calm, direct | The reader wants facts, not apologies | *"We're making improvements. Things should be back by [time]."* |

## Tone decision checklist

Before writing, ask:

1. **What is the reader trying to do?** *(Complete a task, understand something, recover from a problem)*
2. **What emotional state are they likely in?** *(Neutral, frustrated, uncertain, excited, hurried)*
3. **What is the consequence if this goes wrong?** *(No consequence, minor inconvenience, significant impact)*
4. **How much attention will they give this content?** *(Skimming, careful reading, acting under pressure)*

Then locate the content on the two axes:

- High consequence → move toward **formal**
- Frustrated or uncertain reader → move toward **calm** (avoid false cheerfulness)
- Excited or first-use moment → move toward **encouraging**
- Hurried reader → move toward **brief and direct**

## Common tone mistakes

### Applying the wrong tone to the context

Enthusiastic language in an error message reads as dismissive. Formal language on an onboarding screen reads as cold.

| Mistake | Example | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Cheerful error message | *"Oops! Something went wrong 🎉"* | *"Something went wrong. Check your connection and try again."* |
| Apologetic confirmation | *"We're so sorry for the inconvenience, but unfortunately your session has expired."* | *"Your session has expired. Sign in again to continue."* |
| Formal onboarding | *"To commence the account initialisation process, proceed to the next step."* | *"Let's set up your account. This takes about 2 minutes."* |

### False warmth

Warmth should come from genuine helpfulness and human language, not from exclamation marks or performed enthusiasm.

| False warmth | Genuine warmth |
|---|---|
| *"Great! Amazing! Awesome! You did it!!!"* | *"You're in. Your account is ready."* |
| *"We're SO excited to have you here!"* | *"Welcome. Here's how to get started."* |

### Over-apologising

Excessive apology draws attention to the problem, not the solution. Acknowledge clearly, then move forward.

| Over-apology | Appropriate acknowledgement |
|---|---|
| *"We are deeply sorry for any frustration or inconvenience this issue may have caused you."* | *"We're fixing a problem. This won't take long."* |
