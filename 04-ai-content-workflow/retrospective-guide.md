## 1. Purpose and scope

This guide supports reflection after completing a workflow cycle. Its purpose is to help practitioners improve their use of the AI Content Workflow specifically — not to serve as a general project retrospective.

The questions in this guide are directed at the parts of the workflow most likely to benefit from iteration: brief quality, AI use, checkpoint effectiveness, localisation, templates, and revision routing. The goal is practical improvement: better prompts, sharper briefs, more effective checkpoints, and updated templates that reflect what actually worked.

This guide is intentionally lightweight. A retrospective that takes more time than it generates value will not be run consistently. The individual format described here takes around 15 minutes. The team format takes 60–75 minutes.

---

## 2. When to run a retrospective

**After completing a full workflow cycle** — the first retrospective after any new task type, content format, or team composition is particularly valuable, as these represent the most significant source of learning.

**After a task that required substantial rework** — if content returned to an earlier stage more than once, or if a checkpoint revealed significant problems, a retrospective is likely to identify what drove the rework and whether it was preventable.

**Periodically for ongoing work** — teams running the workflow regularly benefit from a brief periodic retrospective (every four to six weeks, or at a natural break point) even when individual tasks have gone smoothly.

A retrospective is not required after every task. Use judgement about where a structured reflection will generate insight versus where a brief informal note is sufficient.

## 3. Individual retrospective

For solo practitioners or for a quick personal reflection after a task.

**Format:** Written reflection or notes. 10–15 minutes.

Work through the following areas, noting what went well, what was harder than expected, and what you would do differently:

**Brief**
- Was the brief complete enough before Stage 03 began?
- Did you need to revisit it during the task? If so, what was missing?

**AI use**
- Where did AI output most need revision or regeneration? What would have improved the initial output?
- Were there places where AI assistance was less useful than expected?

**Checkpoints**
- Did the checkpoints occur at the right points in the work?
- Were there findings at a later checkpoint that an earlier one should have caught?

**Templates and documentation**
- Were the templates you used appropriate for the task? What would you change?
- Was the decision log useful during the task, or did it feel like extra work?

**Revision routing**
- Did any changes after Stage 04 require going back further than expected? What drove that?

After the reflection, note any changes you want to make to your brief template, glossary, or approach for the next task.

## 4. Team retrospective

For teams completing a workflow cycle together, or for a periodic team reflection.

**Format:** Facilitated discussion. 60–75 minutes.

**Suggested structure**

- **Opening (5 minutes):** Confirm the scope of the retrospective — which task or period, which aspects of the workflow you are focusing on.
- **Individual reflection (10 minutes):** Each participant writes notes independently before the discussion. Use the question areas in Section 5. This prevents the discussion from being dominated by whoever speaks first.
- **Discussion by area (30–40 minutes):** Work through the question areas in Section 5. The facilitator captures key themes rather than every comment.
- **Identifying actions (10–15 minutes):** From the discussion, identify specific improvements to make. Each action should be assigned to someone and have a clear outcome (a template change, a glossary addition, a brief section to revise). Do not leave the retrospective with observations that are not attached to changes.
- **Closing (5 minutes):** Confirm the actions and who is responsible for each.

**Facilitation notes**

The retrospective is about improving the framework and how the team uses it — not about assigning blame for things that went wrong. Where a stage or checkpoint was more difficult than expected, the useful question is what would have made it easier, not who was responsible for the difficulty.

If the team is new to running retrospectives, keep the first one to two or three question areas rather than working through all of them. A focused retrospective on AI use, or on the brief and checkpoint process, generates more useful output than a surface pass over everything.

## 5. Retrospective questions by area

Use these as prompts, not a checklist. Not every question will be relevant after every task.

### Brief quality

- Was the brief complete and accurate before Stage 03 began?
- Did approval authority need to be established or clarified during the task rather than before it?
- Were the audience, objective, scope, and requirements clearly enough defined to guide Stage 03?
- If the brief was updated during the task, what triggered the update? Could it have been captured at Stage 02?
- Were the AI use constraints specified in the brief? Were they followed?

### AI use

- At which stages did AI output most need revision or regeneration?
- What grounding materials would have improved the initial output?
- Were there places where the AI-generated content appeared correct but contained errors that only became visible at a later stage?
- Were the Phase 4 AI use exclusions applied consistently?
- Did AI assistance reduce or increase the total effort on this task compared to your expectation?

### Checkpoints

- Did each checkpoint occur at an appropriate point in the workflow?
- Were the right people carrying out each checkpoint?
- Did the Direction, Alignment, Quality, Localisation (including per-locale approval), and Final approval checkpoints remain distinct in practice?
- Were there findings at Quality review or Final approval that earlier checkpoints should have surfaced?
- Did outstanding non-blocking issues documented at earlier stages receive appropriate attention at Final approval?

### Localisation (if applicable)

- Was the source content easy to localise? Were there elements that created unnecessary difficulty in translation?
- Were locale reviewers confirmed and briefed effectively?
- Was the approved glossary complete and correct before localisation began?
- Were source content changes during localisation handled correctly?

### Templates and documentation

- Were the templates useful as starting points, or did they need significant adaptation?
- Was the decision and iteration log maintained consistently throughout the task?
- Did the handoff template capture what the recipient needed?
- Was the final approval checklist complete and accurate?

### Revision routing

- Were any changes after Stage 04 routed to the correct stage?
- Were changes assessed correctly as minor corrections versus substantive revisions?
- Did any changes initially assessed as minor prove to have greater impact than expected?
- Were source content changes during localisation assessed for their impact on locale versions?

## 6. What to capture and where

Retrospective findings that have no record tend not to result in change. Keep the record lightweight enough to be maintained.

**Task-level notes** — Specific observations about the task just completed: what drove a rework, what a checkpoint caught, what the AI output issues were. These can be added to the decision and iteration log for the task as a final entry, or kept as a separate note.

**Template updates** — Changes to the brief template, handoff template, or decision log format based on what was useful or missing. Update the relevant template file and note the change.

**Glossary updates** — New terms identified during the task, corrections to existing entries, or approved translations that were missing. Update the glossary before the next task.

**Systemic observations** — Patterns that recur across tasks (a particular AI grounding approach that consistently underperforms, a checkpoint that is consistently too late in the workflow). These are worth documenting in a shared note or brief retrospective summary that the team can refer to.

Do not create a separate document for every retrospective. A brief note added to the task record and targeted updates to the templates are sufficient for most cycles.

---

## 7. Acting on findings

A retrospective is only useful if its findings result in changes. Apply a simple test to each observation:

- **Is this a one-time issue** specific to this task's context, with no implications for future tasks? Note it in the task record and move on.
- **Is this a recurring issue** or a structural gap in how the team uses the framework? Identify a specific change to make: a template update, a glossary addition, a checkpoint timing adjustment, a brief section to revise.
- **Is this a framework limitation** — an aspect of the framework that does not fit your context? Consider whether the framework can be adapted within its fixed elements, and document the adaptation.

For each change you identify, confirm:
- What specifically will change
- Who will make the change
- By when

Retrospective findings without assigned owners and timelines rarely result in change.
